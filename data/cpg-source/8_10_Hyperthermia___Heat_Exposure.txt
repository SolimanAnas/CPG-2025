CPG 8.10         Hyperthermia / Heat Exhaustion / Heat Stroke

Patient Care Goals
   1. Rapidly identify and manage suspected heat exposure or hyperthermia to prevent progression to severe
      complications like heat stroke.
   2. Provide immediate cooling and supportive care to reduce core body temperature and minimize secondary injury.

Patient Inclusion & Exclusion Criteria
Inclusion Criteria
    • Patients presenting with signs and symptoms suggestive of heat exposure or hyperthermia, including:
          • Exposure to hot environments (e.g., high ambient temperatures, exertion in heat).
          • Clinical features such as elevated body temperature, headache, nausea/vomiting, syncope, heat cramps,
            CNS involvement (e.g., ataxia, confusion, seizures), hot/dry skin, or hypotension.
          • Core temperature above 37°C, often >40°C in heat stroke, as measured or estimated.
          • History of prolonged heat exposure, physical exertion, or inadequate hydration in hot conditions.
Exclusion Criteria
    • Hyperthermia or fever secondary to an intrinsic cause such as sepsis.


Key Patient Assessment Points
   1. Primary and Secondary Survey
        • Perform primary and secondary surveys as per Universal Care CPG.
        • Consider non-environmental causes of hyperthermia (e.g., infections, medications).
   2. Differentiation of Conditions
        • Heat Exhaustion: Elevated body temperature (typically 37–40°C), headache, nausea/vomiting, syncope, heat
          cramps, poor circulation, dehydration.
        • Heat Stroke: Core temperature usually >40°C, neurological dysfunction (delirium, confusion, seizures, loss of
          consciousness), hot/dry skin, hypotension; recognize exertional (from activity) vs. non-exertional (vulnerable
          populations like elderly).
   3. Severity Grading
        • Mild (Heat Cramps/Exhaustion): Localized symptoms without CNS involvement.
        • Severe (Heat Stroke): Systemic effects with CNS dysfunction, potential for multi-organ failure.
   4. Monitoring
        • Assess for dehydration, electrolyte imbalances, and altered mental status.
        • Monitor ECG for arrhythmias secondary to electrolyte imbalances.
   5. Determine Triage Category
        • Determine Triage Category as per Patient Triage Categories CPG.


Patient Management & Interventions
   1. Primary & Secondary Survey
        • Perform Primary and Secondary Surveys as per Universal Care CPG.
   2. Consider ALS back-up
        • Consider the requirement for ALS back-up as per Universal Care CPG.
        • ALS back-up should be routinely requested for:
   3. Initial Care
        • Remove patient from hot environment and prevent further heat exposure.
   4. Cooling Interventions
        • General Cooling:
             • Strip/remove clothing and loosen restrictive garments; spray/mist skin with tepid water; fan to promote
               evaporation; apply ice/cold packs to axilla, groin, and posterior neck.
        • Heat Exhaustion:
             • Passive cooling (remove from heat, fan, remove clothing).
        • Heat Stroke:
             • Aggressive active cooling as priority ("cool first, transport second")

   6. IV Fluid

        • Manage seizures or agitation with benzodiazepines as per Seizures CPG.
   8. Patient Reassessment
        • Frequently Reassess the patient as per Universal Care CPG.


Patient Safety Considerations
   • None Noted


Clinical Insights & Additional Guidance
   • Progression Risk
         • Heat exhaustion can rapidly deteriorate to heat stroke if untreated; focus on rehydration and gentle cooling.
   • Heat Stroke Emergency
         • This is a life-threatening condition with high risk of arrhythmias, electrolyte derangements, seizures, and multi-
           organ failure; aggressive treatment is required.
   • Non-Environmental Hyperthermia
         • Consider intrinsic causes (e.g., malignant hyperthermia, drug toxicity) and manage accordingly.
   • Special Populations
         • Elderly, children, and those with comorbidities are at higher risk; exertional heat stroke common in athletes.
   • Antipyretics
         • Avoid antipyretics (e.g., acetaminophen) as they are ineffective; focus on external cooling.


Documentation
   • Identification: Patient’s full name, age, and gender.
   • Mechanism / Medical Complaint: Description of heat exposure (e.g., environment, exertion), duration.
   • Injuries / Information: Initial temperature (if measured), clinical features, non-environmental causes considered.
   • Signs: Vital signs, neurological status, skin condition, severity classification.
   • Treatment:
         • Cooling methods (e.g., strip/spray/fan/ice, immersion).
         • Fluids administered (PO/IV, type, volume).
         • Any additional interventions (e.g., benzodiazepines for agitation).
         • Hospital notification.
   • Allergies: Known allergies to medications.
   • Medications: Document any administered drugs.
   • Background: Relevant history (e.g., prior heat illness, comorbidities like heart disease).
