CPG 6.4            Anaphylaxis / Allergic Reaction

Patient Care Goals
    1. Rapidly identify and treat life-threatening anaphylaxis or severe allergic reactions
    2. Alleviate symptoms and prevent progression of allergic reactions through timely administration of medications.

Patient Inclusion & Exclusion Criteria
Inclusion Criteria
    • Patients presenting with signs and symptoms of an allergic reaction or anaphylaxis, including:
          • Mild to moderate allergic reaction: Localized urticaria (hives), itching, or mild swelling without systemic
            symptoms.
          • Anaphylaxis: Systemic symptoms involving two or more organ systems (e.g., skin/mucosa, respiratory,
            cardiovascular, gastrointestinal), such as:
                  • Respiratory distress (e.g., wheezing, stridor, shortness of breath).
                  • Hypotension (SBP <90 mmHg in adults, or age-specific for pediatrics).
                  • Angioedema (swelling of face, lips, tongue, or throat).
                  • Gastrointestinal symptoms (e.g., nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain).
          • Known or suspected exposure to an allergen (e.g., food, medication, insect sting, environmental trigger).
          • History of previous allergic reactions or anaphylaxis.
Exclusion Criteria
    • None Noted


Key Patient Assessment Points
    1. Allergen Exposure History
         • Identify recent exposure to potential allergens (e.g., foods, medications, insect stings, latex, or environmental
           triggers).
         • Confirm history of known allergies or previous anaphylactic episodes.
         • Check for medical alert bracelets or documentation indicating allergy history.
    2. Severity Assessment
         • Differentiate mild/moderate allergic reaction (e.g., localized hives) from anaphylaxis (systemic involvement of
           ≥2 organ systems or hypotension).
         • Check for signs of:
                  • Airway compromise (e.g., throat swelling, stridor)
                  • Respiratory Distress (e.g. bronchoconstriction, wheezing)
                  • Shock (e.g., altered mental status, poor perfusion).
    3. Determine Triage Category
         • Determine Triage Category as per Patient Triage Categories CPG.


Patient Management & Interventions
    1. Primary and Secondary Surveys
         • Perform primary and secondary survey as per Universal Care CPG.
    2. Consider ALS back-up
         • Consider the requirement for ALS back-up as per Universal Care CPG.
    3. Consider Red Flags
         • Rapidly progressing respiratory distress or airway obstruction (e.g., stridor, wheezing, or cyanosis).
         • Severe hypotension (SBP <90 mmHg in adults, or age-specific for pediatrics).
         • Altered mental status or unresponsiveness.
         • Persistent or worsening angioedema, especially involving the airway.
         • Known history of severe anaphylaxis
    4. Evaluate for Signs of Shock or Instability
         • Hypotension, tachycardia, or poor perfusion (e.g., cool, clammy skin, delayed capillary refill >2 seconds).
         • If anaphylactic shock is present, prioritize Epinephrine administration and fluid resuscitation.
    5. Establish IV Access

        • Indicated for hypotension, moderate to severe symptoms, or anticipated medication administration.
        • Consider intraosseous (IO) access if IV access cannot be obtained
   6. Fluid Therapy:
        • Administer Normal Saline or Ringers Lactate for signs of shock or dehydration:
                  • Adults: 500-1000 mL bolus IV, reassess, repeat as needed.
                  • Pediatrics: 20 mL/kg bolus IV, reassess, repeat as needed.
           • Monitor for fluid overload, especially in patients with comorbidities.
   7. Mild Reaction
           • Mild reactions do not routinely require treatment.
           • Generally, a recommendation for transport should be given and the patient reassessed en route to hospital.
   8. Moderate Reaction
           • Consider Oxygen
                  • Titrate oxygen to maintain SpO2 ≥94% if hypoxia or respiratory distress is present.
           • Diphenhydramine
                  • Indicated for urticaria, itching, or mild allergic symptoms.
                  • Adults: 25-50 mg IV/IM/PO for mild to moderate allergic reactions or as an adjunct in anaphylaxis.
                  • Pediatrics: 1 mg/kg IV/IM/PO (max 50 mg).
           • Hydrocortisone
                  • Adults: 100-200 mg IV/IM
                  • Pediatrics: 2 mg/kg IV/IM (max 200 mg).
                  • Note: Administer Hydrocortisone after Epinephrine in anaphylaxis cases.
           • If Bronchospasm is present:
                  • Salbutamol
                         • Adults: 2.5-5 mg nebulized for wheezing or bronchospasm.
                         • Pediatrics: 2.5 mg nebulized (for <20 kg) or 5 mg (for ≥20 kg).
                         • Doses should be diluted with normal saline or sterile water up to a maximum volume of 5ml.
                  • Combivent
                         • Adults: 0.5mg Ipratropium Bromide and 3mg Salbutamol (1nebule)
                         • Pediatrics: Not recommended for pediatrics.
                         • Doses should be diluted with normal saline or sterile water up to a maximum volume of 5ml.
   9. Severe Reaction
           • Epinephrine
                  • Adults: 0.3-0.5 mg IM (1:1000) in the anterolateral thigh, repeat every 5-15 minutes as needed for
                    persistent symptoms.
                         • The Adult Epipen is preferred to enable rapid administration
                  • Pediatrics: 0.01 mg/kg IM (1:1000, max 0.3 mg), repeat every 5-15 minutes as needed.
                         • The Pediatric Epipen is preferred to enable rapid administration
           • Additional treatment as per Moderate Reaction
   10. Patient Reassessment
        • Frequently Reassess the patient as per Universal Care CPG.


Patient Safety Considerations
   • Elderly Patients
         • Anaphylaxis may present atypically (e.g., hypotension without skin symptoms).
         • Use caution with Epinephrine in patients with known cardiovascular disease; consult Telemedicine if needed.


Clinical Insights & Additional Guidance
   • Airway Edema
         • Clinicians should have a low threshold for the administration of IM adrenaline in patients with airway edema.
         • Slight increases in airway edema may have a significant impact on airway patency.

         • Invasive airway management may be required if symptoms do not reduce – rapid transfer to hospital is
           required.
         • Ensure Dispatch 2 is informed of any patients with significant airway edema or who do not improve to

Documentation
   • Identification: Patient’s full name, age, and gender.
   • Mechanism / Medical Complaint: Exposure to allergen (e.g., food, insect sting, drug), symptom onset and
     progression.
   • Injuries / Information: History of allergies or anaphylaxis, medical alert tags, medications taken before EMS arrival.
   • Signs: Vital signs, airway status, SpO₂, skin signs (e.g., rash, angioedema), respiratory gastrointestinal or
     cardiovascular symptoms.
   • Treatment:
            • Epinephrine administration: dose, route, time, number of doses.
            • Other medications given: diphenhydramine, hydrocortisone, salbutamol (dose, route, and time).
            • IV fluids: type, volume, and response.
            • Oxygen therapy details.
   • Allergies: Document all known allergies.
   • Medications: Pre-existing medications or recent exposures (e.g., NSAIDs, antibiotics).
   • Background: Previous history of anaphylaxis, asthma, or cardiovascular conditions.
   • Other:
            • Symptom scale (0–10) if used.
            • Communication with Dispatch 2 or Telemedicine.
            • Scene observations or triggers found.


Key Performance Indicators
   1. Administer adrenaline for anaphylaxis within 5 minutes of recognition.
