Patient Care Goals
    • Relieve upper airway obstruction.
    • Reduce patient distress while maintaining oxygenation.
    • Avoid unnecessary agitation that may worsen obstruction.
    • Safely transport to definitive care if required.


Patient Inclusion & Exclusion Criteria
Inclusion Criteria
    • Patients presenting with Croup Symptoms, including;
        • Typically children aged 6 months–6 years.
        • Barking, “seal-like” cough.
        • Inspiratory stridor at rest or with agitation.
        • Hoarseness, respiratory distress.
        • Fever (usually low-grade).
Exclusion Criteria
    • Signs of epiglottitis (drooling, dysphagia, sitting forward, very high fever).
    • Foreign body aspiration (sudden onset, asymmetry).
    • Anaphylaxis.
    • Severe bacterial tracheitis.
    • Asthma (or other causes of bronchospasm)


Key Patient Assessment Points
    1. Primary and Secondary Survey
         • Perform primary and secondary survey’s as per Universal Care CPG.
         • Initial/General Impression
             • Perform from a distance at first, to avoid agitating the child.
             • Use the Pediatric Assessment Triangle (PAT):
                  • Appearance: alertness, tone, interaction, look for lethargy or agitation.
                  • Work of Breathing: stridor, retractions, nasal flaring, tracheal tug, accessory muscle use.
                  • Circulation to Skin: pallor, cyanosis, mottling , signs of hypoxia or poor perfusion.
         • Airway Assessment
             • Listen for barking “seal-like” cough, inspiratory stridor, and hoarseness.
             • Assess for obstructed airway signs:
                  • Drooling (suggests epiglottitis rather than croup).
                  • Difficulty swallowing.
                  • Upper airway noises at rest versus exertion.
             • Assess if stridor is at rest (more concerning) or only on exertion/crying.
         • Breathing Assessment
             • Rate: tachypnea or slow (impending failure).
             • Effort:
                  • Mild: minimal retractions, no stridor at rest.
                  • Moderate: visible retractions (suprasternal, intercostal, subcostal), stridor at rest.
                  • Severe: marked retractions, nasal flaring, fatigue, “silent chest” (air movement reduced).
             • Oxygen saturation (SpO₂): aim for ≥94%; lower indicates hypoxia.
         • Circulation Assessment
             • Heart rate: elevated in distress, bradycardia in severe hypoxia.
             • Capillary refill time: delayed if poor perfusion.
             • Skin color: cyanosis (late, ominous), mottling, pale.
         • Mental Status Assessment
             • Observe for fatigue, reduced level of consciousness, or confusion (signals exhaustion and impending
                respiratory failure).

       • Determine Triage Category as per Patient Triage Categories CPG.
         • Red Priority (Serious & Life-threatening):
               • Severe respiratory distress with hypoxia, lethargy, cyanosis, or impending respiratory failure.

Patient Management & Interventions
   1. Consider ALS back-up
         • Consider the requirement for ALS back-up as per Universal Care CPG.
         • ALS back-up should be routinely requested for:
             • Resting or bi-phasic stridor.
   1. Keep the child calm and minimize distress
           • Maintain a quiet environment, allowing the child to remain with caregivers in a position of comfort, and
             avoiding unnecessary interventions.
           • Avoid procedures or handling that could increase agitation, which may worsen airway obstruction.
   2. If the child exhibits moderate or severe respiratory distress:
           • Croup Severity Score >3 points.
           • Consider nebulized racemic epinephrine (2.25% solution):
                  • Dose: 0.5 mL diluted in 2.5–3 mL of normal saline, administered via nebulizer over ~15 minutes.
                  • May be repeated every 20–30 minutes as needed, observing for improvement and recurrence of
                    symptoms.
                  • Maximum 3 doses without consultation with Tele-EMS.
           • If racemic epinephrine is unavailable, standard L-epinephrine (1:1,000) at 0.5 mL/kg (max 5 mL) nebulized
             may also be used.
           • Any patient who has received nebulized epinephrine must be transported to hospital
   3. Provide supplemental oxygen if SpO2 <94%
           • Target SpO2 ≥94% and <98%, using the least intrusive method tolerated.
           • If SpO₂ falls below 92%, escalate interventions and consider urgent hospital notification.
   4. Prioritize Transport
           • All children receiving racemic epinephrine or who remain in moderate or severe distress should be
             transported promptly to hospital.
   5. Patient Reassessment
           • Frequently Reassess the patient as per Universal Care CPG.
           • Specific Patient Reassessment Points:
                  • Airway:
                      • Any audible change in stridor intensity or loss of audible air movement (potential impending
                        failure).
                  • Breathing:
                      • Work of breathing: stridor at rest, retractions, nasal flaring, tracheal tug, silent chest.
                      • Oxygenation: pulse oximetry (SpO₂) — maintain ≥94%, watch for any downward trend.
                      • Respiratory rate and pattern: monitor for tachypnea, slowing (which may indicate fatigue), irregular
                        breathing.
                  • Circulation:
                      • Heart rate: persistent tachycardia may indicate ongoing distress; bradycardia is a late and serious
                        sign.
                  • Response to treatment:
                      • Response to racemic epinephrine: assess if improvement plateaus or symptoms recur, which may
                        necessitate repeat dosing.
           • Be prepared to intervene if the child’s condition deteriorates, with equipment and medications accessible
             and ready.
           • Ensure clear communication with the receiving facility regarding interventions given, response, and current
             clinical status.

Patient Safety Considerations
   • Avoid unnecessary interventions that increase agitation.
   • Ensure airway equipment is ready but avoid invasive airway procedures unless absolutely necessary.
   • Be prepared for rapid deterioration in severe cases.

Documentation
   • Complete a comprehensive electronic Patient Care Record (ePCR), capturing the following elements to support
    continuity of care. Including:
          • Identification — name, age, gender.
          • Mechanism/Medical complaint — croup: barking cough, stridor.
          • Injuries/Information — fever, severity, any prior episodes or treatments.
          • Signs — vital signs (RR, HR, SpO₂), degree of respiratory distress.
          • Treatment — oxygen, steroids, nebulized adrenaline.
          • Allergies — especially to steroids or adrenaline.
          • Medications — any prior steroids, antipyretics.
          • Background — past medical history (e.g., asthma, previous croup, airway anomalies).
          • Other — caregiver observations, social circumstances, decision to transport or not.


Key Performance Indicators
   1. Administer racemic epinephrine for moderate to severe cases.
   2. Administer oxygen to patients with an SpO₂ <94%

Clinical Insights & Additional Guidance
   • Croup is usually a self-limiting viral illness, but in some cases airway obstruction can progress rapidly.
   • Symptoms often worsen at night and may recur within hours of initial improvement, particularly after the effects of
     racemic epinephrine wear off.
   • Mild cases can often be managed at home with caregiver education, provided the child maintains adequate
     oxygenation and no stridor at rest.
   • Moderate to severe cases require hospital evaluation and monitoring, as repeated doses of racemic epinephrine or
     further interventions may be necessary.
   • Request ALS back-up early if moderate or severe distress, impending failure, or deterioration expected, as per
     Universal Care CPG.
   • Consult Tele-EMS when in doubt about management decisions or disposition.
   • Differentiate croup from other serious upper airway conditions (epiglottitis, bacterial tracheitis, foreign body
     aspiration) which may present similarly but require different management.
   • Ensure caregivers understand the temporary nature of racemic epinephrine’s effect, the potential for symptom
     recurrence, and when to seek further help.
   • Avoid humidified air and unnecessary interventions, as these have not been shown to improve outcomes and may
     increase distress.
   • Always prepare for possible airway compromise and have equipment readily available. Avoid invasive airway
     management unless absolutely necessary.
