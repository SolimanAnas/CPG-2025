CPG 4.8            Starting, Stopping and Transferring CPR

Patient Care Goals
    1. Promptly initiate high-quality CPR (resuscitation) for patients in cardiac arrest.
    2. Make informed decisions about stopping or transferring CPR based on clinical criteria and futility to avoid
       unnecessary prolongation of resuscitation.
    3. Maintain effective CPR during transfer.

Patient Inclusion & Exclusion Criteria
Inclusion Criteria
    • Patients presenting with cardiac arrest, including:
          • Unresponsiveness with no signs of life (e.g., no pulse, no breathing, or agonal gasps).
          • Suspected pulseless electrical activity (PEA), ventricular fibrillation (VF), ventricular tachycardia (VT), or
            asystole.
    • Patients requiring decisions about initiating, continuing, or terminating CPR.
Exclusion Criteria
    • None Noted.


Key Patient Assessment Points
    1. Cardiac Arrest Confirmation
         • Verify unresponsiveness by assessing for response to verbal or painful stimuli (AVPU: U).
         • Check for absence of pulse (carotid or femoral) for 5-10 seconds.
         • Confirm absence of normal breathing or presence of agonal gasps.
    2. Bystander CPR
         • Note the presence (or absence) of bystander CPR; including bystander CPR quality.
    3. Cardiac Rhythm
         • Apply cardiac monitor to identify rhythm (VF/VT/PEA/Asystole).
    4. Time Patient Last Seen Alive
         • Note time of arrest onset or when patient was last seen alive.
    5. Signs of Irreversible Death
         • Assess for signs that indicate incompatibility with life:
                  • Decomposition
                  • Obvious rigor mortis
                  • Obvious pooling (hypostasis)
                  • Incineration
                  • Decapitation
                  • Injuries totally incompatible with life
    6. Futile Resuscitation in Trauma Assessment
           • In the unwitnessed (by EMS) traumatic cardiac arrest patient, assess for signs which indicate that
             resuscitation is not indicated. Including:
                  • Victim of blunt or penetrating trauma
                  • No spontaneous movement
                  • No pupillary response
                  • Apneic and pulseless
                  • ECG shows no organized rhythm


Patient Management & Interventions
    1. Starting CPR
           • CPR does not need to be started on patients who meet the criteria listed below:
                 • Signs of Irreversible Death Assessment
                       • CPR/Resuscitation does not need to be started if any one of the below signs are present:
                             • Decomposition
                             • Obvious rigor mortis

                             • Incineration

                             • Injuries totally incompatible with life
                       • If it is not clear if a sign of irreversible death is present, CPR should be started.

                       • In the unwitnessed (by EMS) traumatic cardiac arrest patient, CPR does not need to be started

          • CPR should be started on all other patients.
          • Once started, CPR should generally be continued for at least 20 minutes.
   2. Transferring CPR
          • Once started, it is mandatory that the below patients are transferred to hospital with on-going CPR:
                 • Mandatory Cardiac Arrest Transfer Criteria:
                         • Age <18 years Old
                         • Drowning Cases
                         • Hypothermia
                         • Pregnancy
                         • A clearly identified reversible cause of cardiac arrest that cannot be corrected in the prehospital

          • Initial resuscitation efforts should be performed prior to transportation (e.g., airway management, BVM
            ventilation, IV access and medication administration).
          • Transportation to hospital should be prioritized in patients where a fast arrival at hospital will provide clear
            benefit to the patient (e.g. cardiac arrest secondary to blood loss).
          • If the patients’ family requests the patient be transferred to hospital, it is appropriate to transfer the patient
            to hospital.
   3. Stopping CPR
          • Once CPR has been started, CPR/resuscitation efforts may only be stopped by a Paramedic, Advanced
            Paramedic or Emergency Physician.
                    • EMT’s cannot stop resuscitation efforts. EMT’s must either transfer the patient to hospital or wait for
                      ALS back-up.
                    • If ALS back-up is significantly far away and additional staff are required to ensure effective CPR
                      during extrication and transfer, another BLS ambulance should be requested if required.
   4. Stopping CPR in the non-trauma patient
          • Paramedics (and higher clinical level) may stop resuscitation efforts in the non-trauma patient if:

Patient Safety Considerations
   • If there is any doubt surrounding starting CPR, CPR should be started.
   • Transportation to hospital should be prioritized in patients where a fast arrival at hospital will provide clear benefit to
     the patient (e.g. cardiac arrest secondary to blood loss).
   • ALS Back Up should be routinely requested for all patients undergoing CPR.
   • If a patient is to be transferred to hospital under CPR, effective resuscitation must be maintained during extrication
     and transfer (chest compressions, airway management, ventilation, medication administration).

Clinical Insights & Additional Guidance
   • Providing Treatment Prior to Transfer
       • In most cases, appropriate initial resuscitation should be performed on-scene prior to starting transfer. This
         includes:
               • Airway management
               • Bag Valve Mask Ventilation
               • Chest Compressions
               • Defibrillation (if required)
               • IV/IO Access and appropriate medication administration
               • Reversal of any identified causes of cardiac arrest

       • If it is difficult to perform some procedures (e.g. IV access) prior to transfer, a clinical decision must be made by
         the staff on scene. This decision must balance the time taken to perform the procedure vs. the time taken to

Documentation
   • Identification: Full patient name, age, and gender.
   • Mechanism / Medical Complaint: Cardiac arrest type, cause (if known), and whether CPR was witnessed or
     unwitnessed.
   • Injuries / Information: Any trauma or clinical signs incompatible with life; bystander CPR provided; last seen alive
     time.
   • Signs: Initial rhythm, EtCO₂ values, response to CPR or ALS interventions, ROSC signs (if any).
   • Treatment:
            • Time CPR started and stopped.
            • Defibrillation details if applicable.
            • Medications administered (type, dose, route, time).
            • Ventilation method and airway management.
            • Transfer decision rationale (e.g., futility or reversible cause).
   • Allergies: Document if known.
   • Medications: Any available pre-arrest medications.
   • Background: Medical history, trauma, or hypothermia indicators.
   • Other: Scene conditions, staff involved, equipment used (e.g., mechanical CPR).
            • Consensus notes for stopping CPR (if applicable).
            • Ensure accurate time-stamping of key actions (CPR start/stop, medication, rhythm changes).
            • Clearly document rationale for transferring or stopping CPR.
            • If CPR is stopped, include signs of irreversible death or all futility criteria met.


Key Performance Indicators
   1. Ensure all relevant information surrounding the decision to start/stop or transfer CPR is captured in the patient
      care report.
