CPG 6.7           Fever & Sepsis

Patient Care Goals
   1. Identify and manage sepsis or septic shock.
   2. Identify patients at risk of sepsis.

Patient Inclusion & Exclusion Criteria
Inclusion Criteria
    • Patients presenting with fever or suspected sepsis, including:
          • Documented or reported fever (temperature >38°C) or hypothermia (<36°C).
          • Symptoms suggestive of infection (e.g., chills, rigors, malaise, or localized symptoms like cough, dysuria, or
            wound infection).
          • Signs of systemic inflammatory response or sepsis, such as tachycardia, tachypnea, hypotension, or altered
            mental status.
          • Suspected septic shock with hypotension (SBP <90 mmHg in adults, or age-specific for pediatrics) and signs
            of poor perfusion.
Exclusion Criteria
    • Fever or symptoms attributable to a non-infectious cause (e.g., heat stroke, or drug reaction).


Key Patient Assessment Points
   1. Infection History
        • Identify potential sources of infection (e.g., respiratory, urinary, skin, or abdominal).
        • Inquire about recent illness, surgery, invasive procedures, or immunocompromised states (e.g., diabetes,
          cancer, or HIV).
        • Confirm history of fever, chills, or rigors, including duration and severity.
   2. Sepsis Screening
        • Assess for systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) criteria:
                • Temperature >38°C or <36°C.
                • Heart rate >90 beats/min (or age-specific for pediatrics).
                • Respiratory rate >20 breaths/min (or age-specific for pediatrics).
                • Altered mental status or confusion.
        • Evaluate for signs of organ dysfunction (e.g., decreased urine output, mottled skin, or hypoxia).
   3. Temperature Assessment
        • Measure body temperature to confirm fever (>38°C) or hypothermia (<36°C).
        • Note subjective reports of fever if measurement is unavailable.
   4. Source Identification
        • Look for localized signs of infection (e.g., erythema, purulent discharge, or cough with productive sputum).
        • Check for medical devices (e.g., catheters, central lines) as potential infection sources.
   5. Determine Triage Category
        • Determine Triage Category as per Patient Triage Categories CPG.


Patient Management & Interventions
   1. Primary and Secondary Surveys
          • Perform Primary and secondary survey as per Universal Care CPG.
          • Consider Oxygen
                • Titrate to maintain SpO2 ≥94% if hypoxia or respiratory distress is present.
   2. Consider Red Flags
        • Hypotension (SBP <90 mmHg in adults, or age-specific for pediatrics) despite fluid resuscitation.
        • Altered mental status or unresponsiveness.
        • Severe respiratory distress or hypoxia (SpO2 <90%).
        • Signs of septic shock (e.g., mottled skin, delayed capillary refill >2 seconds).
        • Immunocompromised patients with fever and systemic symptoms.
   3. Evaluate for Signs of Shock or Instability

         • Hypotension, tachycardia, or poor perfusion (e.g., cool, clammy skin, delayed capillary refill).
         • If septic shock is suspected, initiate aggressive fluid resuscitation and prioritize transport.
   4. Consider ALS back-up
        • Consider the requirement for ALS back-up as per Universal Care CPG.
   5. Establish IV Access
        • Indicated for hypotension, suspected sepsis, or anticipated fluid/medication administration.
        • Consider intraosseous (IO) access if IV access cannot be obtained.
   6. Fluid Resuscitation
        • Administer Normal Saline or Ringers Lactate for signs of sepsis or septic shock:
                • Adults: 500-1000 mL bolus IV (up to 2000mL), repeat as needed.
                • Pediatrics: 20 mL/kg bolus IV, reassess, repeat as needed (max 60 mL/kg).
        • Monitor for fluid overload, especially in patients with cardiac or renal comorbidities.
   7. Paracetamol
        • Indicated to reduce fever and improve patient comfort.
            • Adults: 1 g IV/PO
            • Pediatrics: 15 mg/kg IV/PO (max 1 g).
   8. Consider Vasopressors
        • Consider Adrenaline
        • Indications:
              • Fluid Refractory hypotension.
        • Adrenaline doses should be titrated to a maintain a MAP of >65, the improvement of shock symptoms or the
          return of urine output.
              • IV Push Dose:
                     • Adult: 0.01mg every 2-5 minutes, as required.
                     • Pediatric: 0.005mg/kg every 2-5 minutes, as required.
              • IV Infusion:
                     • Adult: 2-10mcg/min
                     • Pediatric: 0.05-0.3mcg/kg/min
   9. Patient Reassessment
        • Frequently Reassess the patient as per Universal Care CPG.


Patient Safety Considerations
   • Adrenaline Administration:
        • Use a syringe driver wherever possible.
        • IV adrenaline infusions through an IV administration set may be used provided that:
        • Drip rate calculations are cross checked.
        • Extreme caution is exercised to unsure the rate is not mistakenly changed.
        • In children younger than 5 years old, and when a syringe driver is not available, IV push dose adrenaline is
           preferred over IV adrenaline infusion via a drip set.
         • Adrenaline should be reduced in patients with underlying cardiac disease.
   • Transportation Decision Making:
         • Patients who meet SIRS criteria must be recommended transfer to hospital.


Clinical Insights & Additional Guidance
   • Caution should be used when assessing the elderly and pediatrics with fever as some symptoms may not present or
     may be masked by underlying conditions.
   • In the elderly:
          • Classic symptoms such as fever and chills may present as lethargy or decreased appetite
          • Fever response may be impaired
          • Altered mental status may be masked by other diseases such as dementia
          • Tachycardia may be masked by beta-blockers

         • Respiratory rates may already be elevated due to lung disease
         • Baseline hypertension may mask hypotension
         • Clearly identify to dispatch 2 that the patient meets SIRS criteria when coordinating emergency department
           admission.

Documentation
   • Identification: Patient’s full name, age, and gender.
   • Mechanism / Medical Complaint: Symptoms such as fever, chills, altered mental status, hypotension, or signs of
     infection.
   • Injuries / Information: History of recent surgery, device use (e.g., catheter), immunosuppression, or other infection
     risks.
   • Signs: Vital signs (BP, HR, RR, SpO₂), temperature, mental status (AVPU/GCS), signs of poor perfusion (e.g., capillary
     refill, mottled skin).
   • Treatment:
          • IV/IO access placement and fluid resuscitation details (type, volume, response).
          • Paracetamol: dose, route, time.
          • Oxygen therapy and target SpO₂.
          • Adrenaline if given: dose, route, rate, response.
   • Allergies: All known allergies, especially to medications (e.g., paracetamol, antibiotics).
   • Medications: Current or recent use of immunosuppressants or antibiotics.
   • Background: Chronic illness (e.g., diabetes, cancer, renal disease), recent hospitalization.
   • Other:
          • SIRS criteria and shock findings.
          • Dispatch and hospital coordination notes.
          • Document any deterioration or red flag conditions.


Key Performance Indicators
   1. Assess and document SIRS criteria for all suspected sepsis patients.
   2. Assess and document temperature for all suspected sepsis patients.
   3. Administer fluid for hypotension (where appropriate).
