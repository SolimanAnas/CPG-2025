CPG 7.4            Limb Injuries

Patient Care Goals
    • Control external hemorrhage and prevent hemorrhagic shock
    • Stabilize fractures and dislocations to minimize further injury and pain
    • Manage pain effectively while maintaining patient safety
    • Assess and maintain neurovascular function in affected limbs
    • Facilitate timely transport to appropriate facilities for definitive care


Patient Inclusion & Exclusion Criteria
Inclusion Criteria
    • Patients with suspected or confirmed fractures, dislocations, or soft tissue injuries to extremities
    • Traumatic amputations or open wounds with potential for significant bleeding
    • Limb injuries from crush mechanisms or high-energy trauma
    • Neurovascular compromise or compartment syndrome suspicion
    • Scene information indicating mechanism likely to cause limb injury
Exclusion Criteria
    • Limbs which are entrapped. (see Crush Injury CPG).


Key Patient Assessment Points
    1. Initial Impression
            • Scene safety, mechanism of injury, energy involved
            • Number of patients, need for additional resources
    2. Airway & Breathing
            • Ensure airway patency, assess for associated chest trauma
            • Administer oxygen if SpO₂ <94% or respiratory distress
    3. Circulation & Perfusion
            • Check for external bleeding, signs of shock
            • Evaluate pulses, capillary refill, skin color/temperature in affected limb
    4. Neurovascular Status
            • Assess sensation, motor function, distal pulses
            • Check for deformity, swelling, crepitus, open wounds
    5. Compartment Syndrome Signs
            • Pain on passive stretch, pallor, pulselessness, paresthesia, paralysis
    6. Determine Triage Category
            • Determine Triage Category as per Patient Triage Categories CPG.
               • Red Priority: Life-threatening hemorrhage, hypotensive shock, neurovascular compromise.


Patient Management & Interventions
    1. Primary Survey
         • Life-Threatening Injury Identification
               • Detect immediate threats using the C-ABC algorithm (Catastrophic Hemorrhage, Airway, Breathing,
                 Circulation).
               • Focus on catastrophic bleeding, airway obstruction, tension pneumothorax, or signs of shock (e.g.,
                 pallor, delayed capillary refill).
    2. Hemorrhage Control
         • Apply direct pressure, tourniquet if uncontrolled bleeding
         • Use hemostatic agents for junctional wounds
    3. Secondary Survey
         • Perform secondary survey as per Universal Care CPG.
         • Expose effected limb, assess for CMS:
                 • Color
                 • Movement

   4. Pain Management
           • Assess and administer analgesia per Pain Management CPG.
   5. Consider ALS back-up
         • Consider the requirement for ALS back-up as per Universal Care CPG.
         • ALS back-up should be routinely requested for:
           • Severe pain management.
   6. IV Access & Fluid Resuscitation
           • Manage shock as per General Trauma Management CPG.
   7. Splinting and Immobilization
           • Splint fractures/dislocations in anatomic position.
           • Realign if neurovascular compromise, with analgesia.
   8. Limb Amputations
           • Stump Care:
                 • Cover the stump with a moist, sterile dressing.
                 • Apply uniform, moderate pressure using an elastic or compression dressing to control bleeding.
                 • Apply tourniquet if bleeding is not controlled.
           • Amputated Part:
                 • Retrieve amputated part unless it will significantly delay transport.
                 • Amputated parts should be wrapped in moist sterile dressings and placed in a plastic bag.
                 • This should then be surrounded by a bag or container holding a mixture of ice and water.
                 • Do not use ice alone and never use dry ice.
                 • The amputated part must not be allowed to come into direct contact with ice for any length of time.
                 • Cooling part slows chemical processes and will increase viability from 4 hours to up to 18 hours.
   9. Impaled Objects in Extremities
         • Do not remove impaled objects in extremities as it could cause severe hemorrhage.
         • Unnecessary movement of the impaled object outside the body is translated or magnified with the tissues.
         • Secure the object in place with a bulky dressing.
   10. Femur Fractures
         • Traction splints should be routinely applied to isolated mid-shaft femur fractures.
         • Consider ALS back-up for pain associated with applying the traction splint.
         • Application of a traction splint limits blood loss, it is appropriate to delay transfer to apply the traction splint,
           providing there are no other signs or symptoms that require immediate transfer.
         • Traction splints may be used on distal third femur fractures.
             • Due to the proximity to the knee joint, careful assessment is required prior to ensure no knee joint
               involvement.
   11. Dislocations
         • Anterior shoulder dislocations and patella dislocations may be reduced, unless a fracture of the joint is
           suspected.
         • Other types of dislocations should be splinted in place.
         • Pain management for reduction: Penthrox is generally required as a minimum.
               • Anterior Shoulder Reduction Technique
                   • Position the patient: Supine on a stretcher or sitting upright.
                   • Step 1: Elbow Flexion and Traction
                        • Flex the patient’s elbow to 90 degrees.
                        • Apply gentle, steady longitudinal traction along the arm, pulling distally (toward the feet in a
                          supine patient) to disengage the humeral head from the glenoid rim.
                        • Maintain traction throughout Step 2 to prevent muscle spasm.
                   • Step 2: External Rotation
                        • Slowly and gently externally rotate the forearm (move the hand outward, toward the patient’s
                          side) to about 70–90 degrees or until resistance is felt.

                       • Pause if the patient experiences significant pain; allow muscles to relax.
                       • Step 2 should be conducted slowly, allowing muscles to relax
               • Lateral Patella Reduction Technique
                   • Place the patient supine on a stretcher with the hip flexed slightly (e.g., 20–30 degrees) to relax the
                     quadriceps muscle.
                   • Alternatively, the patient may sit with the leg supported if more comfortable.
                   • Step 1: Knee Extension
                       • Gently extend the knee by slowly straightening the leg. This relaxes the quadriceps muscle and
                         aligns the patella with the femoral groove.
                       • Apply mild pressure to the shin or foot to assist extension, avoiding forceful manipulation.
                   • Step 2: Apply Medial Pressure
                       • While extending the knee, place your hand or thumb on the lateral edge of the patella.
                       • Apply gentle, steady pressure to push the patella medially (toward the midline) back into the
                         femoral groove.
                       • The patella often reduces with a palpable or audible clunk as it slides back into place.
   12. Patient Reassessment
        • Frequently Reassess the patient as per Universal Care CPG.
        • Specific Patient Reassessment Points:
               • Limb CMS (colour, movement and sensation) must be reassessed:
                   • Immediately after splinting.
                   • Every 15 minutes.


Patient Safety Considerations
   • Ensure scene safety before approaching patient
   • Avoid manipulation without analgesia if possible
   • Reassess neurovascular status after splinting
   • Use caution with tourniquets; document time applied
   • Prevent hypothermia by covering patient
   • Be prepared for deterioration during transport


Documentation
   • Identification: Age, gender, weight
   • Medical complaint: Limb injury description, mechanism
   • Injuries/Information: Affected limb, deformity, neurovascular status
   • Signs: Vitals, pain score, bleeding assessment
   • Treatment: Hemorrhage control, splinting, analgesia
   • Allergies: If known
   • Medications: Analgesics, fluids administered
   • Background: Comorbidities, anticoagulants
   • Other: Time of injury, amputated part management


Clinical Insights & Additional Guidance
   • The use of a tourniquet for uncontrolled limb bleeding is exceptionally important. Unsure that torniquets are applied
     when indicated.
   • Manipulation of fractured limbs to apply a splint will likely increase pain. Ensure adequate analgesia is administered
     prior to splinting.

Key Performance Indicators
   1. Apply a traction splint for mid-shaft fracture femurs, when no time critical injuries are present.
